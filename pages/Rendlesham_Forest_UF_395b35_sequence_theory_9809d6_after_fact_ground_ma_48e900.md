## Introduction

One of the most frequently cited pieces of physical evidence in the [Rendlesham Forest]({{ 'rendlesham-forest/' | relative_url }}) incident is the discovery of marks on the forest floor after the [first night]({{ 'first-night/' | relative_url }})’s sightings. In many retellings, these depressions are presented as the place where an unidentified craft landed. Yet the marks did not become “landing evidence” at the moment they were found. They acquired that meaning only after witnesses had already been searching for an explanation for the lights seen in the forest.

In that interpretation, the ground traces were not an independent proof that a craft had landed. Instead, they were ambiguous features discovered after personnel had begun treating the area as the location of a possible crash or landing. These observations later became central to claims that a physical object had touched down in the clearing. ## How Expectation Changes Interpretation

The sequence theory asks a different question: what would investigators have thought about those same marks if nobody had reported a strange object beforehand?

Viewed in isolation, three shallow depressions in a forest clearing do not automatically indicate a landing site. Their significance depended heavily on context. By the time the marks were examined, military personnel had already spent hours discussing unusual lights and searching the area where they believed something had descended. The clearing was therefore not a neutral location. It was already suspected to be the destination of whatever had been seen during the night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFO reportsMost reports refer to lights being spotted, rather than an actual ship or craft. Psychologists often describe this process as expectation-driven interpretation. People naturally evaluate ambiguous evidence through the lens of what they already believe may have happened. In the Rendlesham case, once a landing scenario was under consideration, ordinary ground disturbances could be viewed as confirming that scenario rather than merely existing alongside it.

This does not require dishonesty or irrationality. It is a common feature of investigations. A clue discovered after a theory has formed can appear much more meaningful than the same clue would have appeared beforehand. The marks therefore became persuasive partly because they were discovered within an already developing story of a craft in the woods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sitp.online/show/the-rendlesham-forest-ufo-deconstructing-a-myth-ian-ridpath/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sitp.online">[Skeptics in the Pub Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sitp.online</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Skeptics in the Pub OnlineThe Rendlesham Forest UFO: Deconstructing a myth - Ian...This talk looks at the main points of the case and ex...</span></span></span>

## Why Ordinary Marks Can Look Connected

The strongest sceptical challenge to the landing-mark interpretation concerns the origin of the depressions themselves.

Local police who examined the site reportedly regarded the impressions as unremarkable and suggested they could have been caused by animals. Subsequent investigations by sceptical researchers highlighted testimony from forestry personnel who considered the depressions consistent with rabbit scrapings or rabbit diggings commonly found in the area. The alleged burn marks on nearby trees were also interpreted by forestry workers as ordinary [axe cuts]({{ 'axe-cuts/' | relative_url }}) used in woodland management rather than evidence of intense heat or propulsion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia+2Military Wiki]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">forest and found three small impressions on the ground, as well as burn marks and broken branches on nearby trees. The crucial point is not that every sceptical explanation has been universally accepted. Rather, it is that the traces were not uniquely diagnostic of a landing craft. Multiple ordinary explanations existed for the same physical features. If a forest contains animal disturbances, cut trees, broken branches, and uneven ground—as many working forests do—then some combination of those features can appear highly significant when observers are actively searching for evidence of an unusual event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ianridpath.com/ufo/rabbits.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ianridpath.com">[Ian Ridpath+2Metabunk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ianridpath.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ian RidpathRendlesham Forest UFO case – Rabbit scrapingsIn his memo, Col Halt described the marks he saw as seven inches across and 1.5 i...</span></span></span>

Critics saw them as ordinary forest features retrospectively connected to those reports. ## From Forest Features to Landing Site

The landing marks occupy a distinctive place in the Rendlesham story because they sit at the boundary between observation and interpretation. Everyone broadly agrees that some kind of ground disturbance was found. The dispute concerns what transformed those disturbances into evidence of a landed craft.

Within the sequence theory, the answer is timing. The marks were discovered after reports of lights had already directed attention toward a particular area of forest. Once that happened, the depressions, tree damage, and other features could be assembled into a coherent landing narrative.
